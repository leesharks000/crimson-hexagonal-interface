> Brief Preface to Day and Night: Conversations with Sapphic Desire > > This work is a collection and arrangement of translations of ancient Greek lyric poets, drawn heavily from Sappho but also including other canonical melic poets (Alcman, Anacreon, Simonides, and a few others), and one Roman poet, Catullus. > > Sappho was a Greek poet from the island of Lesbos who lived from around 630 B.C. Her poems were initially composed to be performed to music. She is the most well-known and celebrated of the ancient lyric poets, both now and with her contemporaries. An epigram in the Palatine Anthology (9.506), ascribed -- probably erroneously -- to Plato, sums up her reputation by calling her “the tenth Muse.” A few of Sappho's most common themes are love and desire, death and aging, loss, relationships and community, poetry, and religious rites and invocations. Of the nine books of her poetry that scholars in Alexandria collected, we have merely scraps, fragments, quotations, and a stray poem or two. We might divide Sappho's extant work into several categories: the few poems that are nearly complete, or complete enough to get a sufficient sense of the original poem or some significant portion of it; complete stanzas, not enough to get a sense of the whole poem, but a stanza complete in itself, often quoted in an external ancient source; longer fragmentary pieces, often from preserved papyrus manuscripts; shorter fragments; a single line or word. The fragmentary nature of the remnants poses peculiar challenges and offers up peculiar joys to the translator. > > The subtitle of the collection, Conversations With Sapphic Desire, speaks to the way in which the collection might be conceived as a series of calls and responses to the shape of desire in Sappho's poetry, and the way that Sapphic desire moves and echoes through a tradition. The inclusion of Catullus, although it violates the consistency of the choice to include mostly Greek poets, and is separated from them temporally by hundreds of years, is one example of the way that Sapphic desire remains a vibrant and evolving force in Roman poetry and beyond. Although the arrangement, at the present moment, is a first draft -- provisional and rough -- I conceive it as telling the narrative, not of a life, but of an affect, the movement of desire from its dawn to its extinction in death. This affective biography at the same time intersects with a number of lives, and the whole arrangement proceeds under the stellar sign of the progression from day to night, from the first poem, in which the sun rises, to the final section, which is plunged into darkness and death. > > I have attempted to translate the poems in a flowing, idiomatic modern style, and my goal at every point has been to create translations that read as excellent poems in English. Walter Benjamin, in “The Task of the Translator,” writes, “the task of the translator consists in finding that intended effect upon the language into which he is translating which produces in it the echo of the original.” What kind of object of knowledge is an “original”? How do we have access to it if not via our subjective experience of reading, and to what degree is any translation bound to be as much a translation and reproduction of that subjective experience, rather than a translation of an objective “original”? > > For Benjamin, the unessential, the poetic, and the non-communicative in a source-text are the primary objects of the “good” translator. It seems to me that this poetic element is precisely the subjective, aesthetic element, to which one would have access, not through an objective standard of transparent communication or the transmission of information, but through his own subjective experience of reading. And yet this maximally subjective element---this element that is coterminous with the subjective, that is somehow peculiarly in excess of the communicable (the universal)---is precisely the universal element, the element in a poem that, for Benjamin, > > persists beyond the disaster of translation, that passes through the desert of impossibility. So we've arrived at a strange impasse, wherein the absolutely particular and untranslatable is coterminous with the absolutely universal -- the untranslatable is the only element that will make it across the desert of translation. > > If all translation is a betrayal, then it is my conviction that the most heinous betrayal is taking a poem that, in its source language, is excellent, and turning it into a poem that, in the target language, is stilted, mediocre, and unexceptional. It is in that spirit that I have given the best I have to offer in maintaining fidelity to the originals.

Sappho 1, a new translation

I'm begging you, Aphrodite: Zeus-

born immortal of light-

dappled thrones: don't

abandon me---but come.

If ever you've heard

my cries and quit

your father's golden house

in sparrow-drawn chariots, bright

wings cutting above black

earth, a blur

of speed from the middle-

horizon: they came---

and you, goddess: depthless

eyes smiling, you asked

what's happened, this time, and why

do I call on you, this time? What

does my frenzied heart need

so badly? "What sweet girl

will crawl to your arms? Sappho,

love, who's hurt you? If the girl

strays, she'll soon

give chase; or spurns

your gifts, she'll give them.

Though she feels

no desire, she'll burn

for you soon."

So come to me. Drain

tension from my

troubled thoughts,

whatever my heart

wants---do it.

Once again be in battle beside me.
